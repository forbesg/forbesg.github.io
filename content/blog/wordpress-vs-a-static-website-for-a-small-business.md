If you're getting a new website, sooner or later someone will ask whether you want
WordPress. It runs a huge share of the web, so it's often the default choice. But it's
not the only option, and for many small businesses it isn't the best one.

I build most sites as static websites, so I'm not neutral here. But I'll be honest about
where WordPress is still the better fit.

## What's the difference?

**WordPress** builds each page when someone asks for it. Your content lives in a
database, and the server puts the page together on the fly using your theme and
plugins. That's what lets you log in and change things, but it also means there's
software running behind your site all the time, which needs looking after.

**A static website** is built in advance. Every page is created once, when the site is
published, and then served as a ready-made file. There's no database or server code
running when a visitor arrives, so there's very little that can slow down or go wrong.

"Static" doesn't mean you can't change it. You can still edit your own pages, news and
menus through an easy content editor (a headless CMS), and the site rebuilds itself
when you hit publish.

## Speed

Static sites are fast almost by default, because the pages are ready before anyone asks
for them and can be served from a network of servers close to your visitors.

WordPress sites can be fast too, but it takes work: good hosting, caching, and keeping
the number of plugins down. Many small business WordPress sites end up slow because of
a heavy theme or a pile of plugins added over the years. Speed matters, because most
local searches happen on a phone, and people don't wait.

## Security and upkeep

Because WordPress is so popular, it's a common target for attacks, and most of the risk
comes from outdated plugins and themes. To keep a WordPress site safe, someone needs to
apply updates regularly, and occasionally an update breaks something.

A static site has far less to attack: no database behind the page and no login on the
public site. There are no plugins to keep updated, so there's much less ongoing
maintenance.

## Editing your own content

This is where WordPress has a real strength. Its editor is familiar to a lot of people,
and you can add almost anything through a plugin.

A static site with a headless CMS gives you an editor that's built around your content:
your services, your menu, your team, your news, with fields that make sense for your
business. It's usually simpler to use, because it only shows you what you need, and you
can't accidentally break the layout.

If you'll only change your site a few times a year, you might not need an editor at
all. A simple [brochure website](/services/brochure-websites/) keeps costs down, and I
can make the odd change for you.

## Running costs

A WordPress site needs hosting that can run PHP and a database, and premium themes and
plugins often come with yearly licences. Then there's the time, or the cost, of keeping
it all updated.

Static sites can be hosted very cheaply, and with me hosting is free for the first
year. Many headless CMSs have free or very low cost plans for small businesses. My post
on [how much a website costs in Scotland](/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost-in-scotland/)
sets out the running costs to budget for.

## When WordPress is the better choice

WordPress still makes sense when:

- **You need a specific plugin** that does exactly what your business needs and would
  be expensive to build from scratch.
- **You have a team who already know WordPress** and want to keep using it.
- **Your site changes constantly**, with many people publishing every day, like a news
  site or a large blog.

## When a static website is the better choice

For most small businesses, a static site is the better fit when:

- **You want it fast**, on phones and on Google.
- **You don't want to think about updates**, security patches or broken plugins.
- **You update your site now and then**, rather than every hour: new offers, a menu, a
  news post or a few photos.
- **You want low running costs**, without yearly plugin licences.
